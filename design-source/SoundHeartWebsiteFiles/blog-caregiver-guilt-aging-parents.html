The Guilt of Not Doing Enough: Caring for Aging Parents When You're Stretched Thin

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 6 min read

You visit, you call, you manage the appointments and the medications and the crises, and still, at the end of the day, a voice whispers that it isn't enough. Caregiver guilt is one of the most common and least talked-about burdens of caring for aging parents. It is also, almost always, deeply unfair to the person carrying it.

Why guilt shows up

Guilt tends to arrive precisely because you care. It shows up when you can't be in two places at once, when you feel impatient, when you long for your old life, or when you feel relief at a break. None of that makes you a bad son or daughter. It makes you a human being with finite time, energy, and a life of your own.

The feelings no one says out loud

  • Resentment, toward the situation, siblings who help less, even your parent. Common, and not the same as not loving them.
  • Grief, you may be mourning the parent they used to be, even as they're still here. That's anticipatory grief.
  • Relief, at a quiet hour, or eventually at an ending. Relief and love can sit side by side.

Guilt is not a to-do list

Guilt tells you that if you just did more, you'd feel okay. But it doesn't work that way, there is no amount of doing that satisfies it, because the problem isn't your effort. Treating these feelings as human rather than shameful is what lets you carry them instead of being crushed by them.

Caring for yourself is part of caregiving

The "sandwich generation", caring for parents and often children at once, runs a real risk of burnout. Your own rest, support, and limits aren't a betrayal of your parent. They're what allow you to keep showing up with something left to give.

Common questions

Why do I feel so guilty caring for my aging parent?

Guilt usually arises because you care and because you're human and finite. Feeling impatient, resentful, or relieved for a break doesn't make you a bad child, those feelings are normal responses to an overwhelming role.

Is it normal to resent caring for a parent?

Yes. Resentment toward the situation, unequal help from siblings, or even your parent is extremely common and doesn't cancel out love. Naming it honestly is healthier than hiding it.

How do I deal with caregiver burnout?

Treat your own rest, support, and limits as part of caregiving, not a betrayal of it. Counseling, respite, and support from others help you keep showing up without running yourself into the ground.
