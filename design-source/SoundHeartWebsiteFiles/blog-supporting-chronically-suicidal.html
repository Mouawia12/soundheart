Supporting Someone You Love

Loving Someone Who Is Chronically Suicidal: How to Help Without Carrying It Alone

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 6 min read

If you are in crisis, please don't wait. Call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or dial 911. SoundHeart is not a crisis or emergency service. This article is educational and is not a substitute for individual clinical care.

Loving someone who lives with ongoing suicidal thoughts is exhausting, frightening, and profoundly lonely. Your presence genuinely matters, and at the same time, you cannot be their only lifeline, and you are not responsible for keeping another adult alive by yourself. Both of these things are true.

What actually helps

You don't need the perfect words. What helps most is showing up honestly and without panic:

  • Take it seriously and stay calm. Being able to talk openly about it is protective, not dangerous.
  • Ask directly and listen without rushing to fix: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Asking does not plant the idea.
  • Remind them their life matters to you, specifically. "My life would be less without you in it."
  • Help them connect to professional care, and stay connected to that care team yourself.

Safety, guided by professionals

For someone who is chronically suicidal, a professional-led safety plan is far better than trying to police risk on your own. Let their therapist or care team guide decisions about safety at home and what to do in a crisis, so the weight isn't resting entirely on you.

You cannot pour from empty

Here's the part loved ones most often skip: your own well-being is not optional. Caregiver burnout is real, and running yourself into the ground helps no one. Your own therapy, your own support system, and permission to have a life outside of caregiving are what let you keep showing up. Supporting someone long-term is a marathon, and even marathoners rest.

In an immediate crisis

If your loved one is in immediate danger, don't wait: call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), or dial 911. Stay with them if you safely can until help arrives.

If you're also supporting someone through psychosis or addiction, know that these often overlap, and so does the toll on you.

Common questions

Does asking someone about suicide make it worse?

No. Research consistently shows that asking directly and compassionately does not plant the idea, it can bring relief and open the door to help. Being able to talk about it openly is protective.

How do I help someone who is chronically suicidal?

Take it seriously, ask directly, listen without panic, remind them specifically that they matter to you, and help them stay connected to professional care. Just as importantly, get support for yourself so you're not carrying it alone.

What if it's an immediate emergency?

If your loved one is in immediate danger, call or text 988 or dial 911, and stay with them if you safely can until help arrives.
