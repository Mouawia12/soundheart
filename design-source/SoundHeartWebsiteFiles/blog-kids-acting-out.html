When Kids Act Out: Is It Behavioral or Psychological? A Parent's Guide to Getting Help

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 7 min read

When your child is melting down, defiant, or withdrawn, and nothing you try is working, one exhausting question keeps circling. Is this a behavior problem, or is something deeper going on? The honest answer is that it is often both, and the good news is that there is a whole range of help, and a way to find the right level for your child.

Behavioral or psychological, or both

Acting out is almost always communication. A child who cannot say I am anxious, I am grieving, I feel out of control, or something happened to me will often show it through behavior instead. So the question is less discipline versus therapy and more, what is this behavior trying to tell us? Behavior and emotion are deeply linked, and lasting change usually means addressing the feeling underneath, not just the behavior on top.

Signs it is worth seeking help

  • The behavior is intense, frequent, or lasting, and ordinary parenting tools are not working.
  • It is hurting your child's friendships, school, or family life.
  • There are signs of anxiety, depression, trauma, or self-harm.
  • Your gut tells you something is wrong. Parents are usually right to trust that.

Understanding levels of care

Mental health help for kids comes in levels, and matching the level to the need is key. From least to most intensive, these generally include outpatient therapy, such as play therapy or individual and family sessions, then more frequent intensive outpatient and day treatment programs, and at the highest end, residential treatment where a child lives on site for a time. Most children never need the higher levels. A good clinician can help you find the least intensive option that truly meets your child's needs.

Finding resources, in state and beyond

Alaska has outpatient and community mental health options, and for higher levels of care, some families use programs both in state and out of state. Your pediatrician, your child's school counselor, and a therapist can help you navigate referrals, and if cost is a concern, ask about what insurance covers at each level. If you are ever facing an emergency or worried about your child's safety, call or text 988, or dial 911.

Caring for the whole family

Parenting a high needs child is one of the hardest and loneliest jobs there is, and doing it while nothing seems to work can wear you down completely. You matter too. Family support, respite, and your own care are not extras, they are what let you keep going. Our writing on breaking the cycle and caregiver guilt speaks to that weight.

Common questions

Is my child's behavior a discipline problem or a mental health issue?

Often both. Acting out is usually communication of a feeling a child cannot express in words. Lasting change generally means addressing the emotion underneath, such as anxiety, grief, or trauma, not just the behavior itself.

What are the levels of care for children's mental health?

From least to most intensive, they generally include outpatient therapy, intensive outpatient and day programs, and residential treatment. Most children never need the higher levels, and a clinician can help match the level to the need.

Where can I find help for my struggling child in Alaska?

Start with your pediatrician, school counselor, and a therapist, who can guide referrals and outpatient care. For higher levels of care, some families use in state and out of state programs. In an emergency, call or text 988 or dial 911.
