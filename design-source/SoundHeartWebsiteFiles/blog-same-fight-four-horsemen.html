Couples & Relationships

Why You Keep Having the Same Fight, and the Four Patterns That Predict Trouble

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 6 min read

Poor communication is the single most common reason couples seek therapy, and the "same fight on repeat" is usually less about the topic than about a handful of predictable patterns. The encouraging part: those patterns were learned, which means they can be re-practiced.

Most couples don't fight about a hundred different things. They have two or three fights, over and over, wearing the same grooves deeper each time. And here's a sobering number: couples wait, on average, about six years after serious problems begin before they reach out for help. A lot of avoidable damage happens in those years.

The Four Horsemen

Relationship researcher John Gottman identified four communication patterns so corrosive he called them the "Four Horsemen," because their steady presence predicts relationship breakdown:

  • Criticism, attacking your partner's character ("you always…") instead of naming a specific behavior.
  • Contempt, mockery, eye-rolling, sarcasm, disgust. The single strongest predictor of divorce.
  • Defensiveness, meeting a complaint with counter-blame instead of taking any responsibility.
  • Stonewalling, shutting down and withdrawing, usually because the body is flooded and overwhelmed.

Why it feels impossible to stop

These patterns aren't character flaws, they're what a nervous system does when it feels unsafe with someone it loves. That's the core of the NeuroRelational view: the reactions were conditioned, not chosen, which is exactly why deliberate practice can change them.

What changes the cycle

The antidotes are learnable: softening how you raise an issue, building a culture of appreciation, taking responsibility for even a small part, and learning to notice when you're flooded and take a real break before you stonewall. When the same fight has hardened into a crisis, an intensive can help you interrupt it faster than weekly sessions.

Common questions

What are the Four Horsemen in a relationship?

A term from researcher John Gottman for four communication patterns that predict relationship breakdown: criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling. Contempt is the strongest single predictor of divorce.

Why do we keep having the same argument?

Recurring fights are usually driven by underlying patterns and unmet needs rather than the surface topic. When those patterns aren't addressed, the same conflict resurfaces in different clothing.

Can communication patterns actually change?

Yes. These patterns are learned responses, not fixed traits, and they respond well to deliberate practice, which is the foundation of the work we do with couples.
