Grieving Someone Who's Still Here: The Long Goodbye and Anticipatory Grief

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 6 min read

Grief doesn't always wait for death. When a loved one is facing a terminal illness or a slow decline, the mourning often begins long before the final goodbye, quietly, and often without recognition. This is anticipatory grief: the very real experience of grieving someone who is still here.

You can miss someone who's still alive

With dementia, a terminal diagnosis, or a long decline, families grieve in real time, mourning each small loss as it happens. The parent who used to remember your birthday. The partner who feels like a stranger some days. There's no funeral for these losses, no ritual, no one giving you permission to grieve. That's exactly what makes anticipatory grief so isolating. Your grief is real anyway.

The tangle of feelings

  • Sadness and love, right alongside exhaustion and dread.
  • Guilt, over impatience, over a wish for it to be over, over stepping away for an hour.
  • Relief that coexists with grief. Both can be true; neither cancels the other.

Guilt is one of the most common and most hidden parts of this experience, especially for adult children who have become caregivers to their own parents.

It doesn't mean you've given up

Grieving now is not the same as abandoning hope, and it doesn't mean you love them any less. It means you love someone whose life, and your relationship with them, is changing in real time.

Why naming it matters

Families who begin processing grief before a loss often fare better afterward. Naming the experience, seeking support, and having honest conversations can reduce the risk of complicated grief and unresolved guilt later. The love underneath a long goodbye deserves the same care as any other grief, including grief after a death.

Common questions

What is anticipatory grief?

It's the grief that begins before a loss, while a loved one is still alive but facing terminal illness or decline. It's common among family members and caregivers and is just as real as grief after death.

Is it normal to grieve someone before they die?

Yes. Mourning the losses as they happen, and feeling sadness, guilt, and even relief all at once, is a normal response to a long goodbye, and it doesn't mean you've given up hope.

Does grieving early mean I love them less?

No. Anticipatory grief is a sign of how deeply you love someone whose life is changing in real time. It's not abandonment or giving up.
