Getting Help for Addiction: Levels of Care, Resources, and Preparing for Recovery

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 7 min read

If you or someone you love is ready to get help for addiction, the range of options can feel overwhelming. Here is a map. Treatment comes in levels of care matched to need, resources exist both in state and beyond, and lasting recovery involves not just stopping the substance, but healing the pain underneath and changing the life around it.

Understanding levels of care

Addiction professionals often use a framework from the American Society of Addiction Medicine, known as the ASAM levels of care, to match treatment intensity to a person's needs. From least to most intensive, these generally include:

  • Outpatient counseling. Regular therapy while living at home, for milder situations or ongoing support.
  • Intensive outpatient and day programs. Several hours of structured treatment most days, while still living at home.
  • Residential or inpatient treatment. Living on site for a period of focused, round the clock care.
  • Medically managed detox. Supervised withdrawal when stopping a substance is medically risky, often a first step before other care.

Matching the level to the need matters. A good assessment helps find the least intensive option that will actually work.

Finding resources, in state and out

Alaska has outpatient providers, community programs, and support meetings, and for higher levels of care some people travel to programs out of state. Starting points include your doctor, a licensed counselor, and national directories such as the SAMHSA helpline at 1-800-662-4357, which is free, confidential, and available at all hours. If cost is a worry, ask each program what insurance covers at that level.

Preparing to recover

Recovery is not only about willpower. It is about changing the environment that surrounds the addiction. In recovery this is often summed up as people, places, and things, the relationships, locations, and habits that trigger use. Preparing means honestly looking at which people, places, and routines support recovery and which pull against it, and building new ones that do not. Recovery grows a new life, not just a substance free version of the old one.

Healing the underlying cause

For most people, addiction began as a way to cope with something, trauma, anxiety, depression, grief, or unbearable stress. Stopping the substance without healing that pain often leaves the door open to relapse. That is why lasting recovery pairs treatment for the addiction with real work on what drove it, which is where therapy comes in. As we explore in why addiction is so hard to quit, treating the wound underneath is what makes recovery hold, and family support strengthens it further.

Common questions

What are the ASAM levels of care for addiction?

A framework from the American Society of Addiction Medicine that matches treatment intensity to need, generally ranging from outpatient counseling, to intensive outpatient and day programs, to residential treatment, to medically managed detox.

What does people, places, and things mean in recovery?

It refers to the relationships, locations, and habits that can trigger substance use. Preparing for recovery means honestly examining which support sobriety and which pull against it, then building new, healthier ones.

Why is treating the underlying cause important?

Addiction usually began as a way to cope with trauma, anxiety, depression, or stress. Stopping the substance without healing that pain often leads to relapse, so lasting recovery pairs treatment with therapy for what drove it.
