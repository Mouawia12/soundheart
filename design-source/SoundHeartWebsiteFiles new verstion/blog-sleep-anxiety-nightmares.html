Sleep Anxiety and Nightmares: Breaking the Cycle of Restless Nights

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 5 min read

When you lie down exhausted and your mind switches on, or when sleep itself feels unsafe because of the nightmares waiting there, bedtime can become the most stressful part of your day. Here is the trap and the way out. The fear of not sleeping is what keeps you awake, and that cycle can be broken.

How the cycle works

Sleep anxiety feeds itself. A few bad nights lead to dread about the next one. You start watching the clock, trying to force sleep, and bracing for failure, all of which flood the body with the exact alertness that makes sleep impossible. The harder you try, the more awake you become. The problem is no longer the original bad night. It is the anxiety about sleep itself.

When nightmares are the fear

For people carrying trauma, the fear is often the nightmares. The mind replays frightening material at night, so sleep starts to feel dangerous, and avoidance and exhaustion pile up. This is common and treatable, and it is not something you have to simply endure.

What actually helps

  • Approaches based on cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, which retrain the brain to associate bed with sleep rather than dread.
  • Letting go of the effort to force sleep, since trying harder backfires.
  • For trauma nightmares, specific techniques that help rewrite the recurring dream and calm the nervous system.
  • Treating the underlying anxiety, since a calmer day supports a calmer night.

Rest is possible again

You do not have to organize your life around the fear of another sleepless night. Addressing the anxiety and any underlying trauma, sometimes with tools like EMDR, can give you back your nights. If daytime panic is part of the picture, our guide on panic attacks can help too. In the long Alaska winters, protecting sleep matters even more, as we cover in recognizing seasonal depression.

Common questions

Why does trying to sleep make my insomnia worse?

Because effort creates pressure and alertness. A few bad nights lead to dread, clock watching, and forcing sleep, which floods the body with the very arousal that prevents it. The fear of not sleeping becomes the real problem.

Can therapy help with nightmares?

Yes. For trauma related nightmares, specific techniques help rewrite the recurring dream and calm the nervous system, and treating the underlying trauma, sometimes with EMDR, reduces them at the source.

What is the best treatment for sleep anxiety?

Approaches based on cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia are highly effective. They retrain the brain to associate bed with sleep, reduce the pressure to force it, and address the underlying anxiety.
