Supporting Someone You Love

How to Support a Loved One Through Psychosis: What to Say

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 6 min read

If you are in crisis, please don't wait. Call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or dial 911. SoundHeart is not a crisis or emergency service. This article is educational and is not a substitute for individual clinical care.

Watching someone you love lose contact with reality is one of the most frightening experiences a family can face. You can't argue someone out of a delusion, but the way you respond can lower fear, build trust, and help them accept care. Here's what tends to help, and what to avoid.

Psychosis isn't a diagnosis in itself; it's a symptom of several conditions, in which someone loses some contact with reality, hearing, seeing, or believing things that aren't shared by others. To them, those experiences feel completely real and often terrifying. That's the starting point for how to help.

Focus on the person, not the delusion

You don't have to confirm a belief you don't share, and you don't have to argue it away either. Instead, connect with the feeling underneath. A version of the LEAP approach, Listen, Empathize, Agree where you can, Partner, works well:

  • Listen without judgment: "I hear you saying people are following you, and that sounds scary."
  • Empathize with the emotion: "That must be frightening. I'd be scared too."
  • Stay calm and simple, short sentences, gentle tone, no crowding.
  • Partner toward care: "I care about you. Let's find someone who can help you feel safer."

Encourage professional care

Psychosis responds to treatment, and a steady relationship with a therapist and prescriber matters enormously. As a family member, you can be part of that team, helping track triggers and stay consistent, while leaving diagnosis and medication to professionals.

When it's a crisis

If your loved one is at immediate risk of harming themselves or someone else, treat it as an emergency: call or text 988, or dial 911. When you call, describe what you see plainly and ask for help. If police respond, tell them it's a mental health crisis.

Don't forget yourself

Supporting someone with serious mental illness is exhausting and lonely. Your own steadiness is part of the care they receive, which is why tending to your own well-being isn't selfish. It's essential.

Common questions

What should I say to someone experiencing psychosis?

Focus on their feelings rather than debating their beliefs. Listen, empathize ('that sounds frightening'), stay calm and use short sentences, and gently partner with them toward professional help. You don't have to confirm or argue against a delusion.

Should I argue with their delusions?

No. Arguing usually increases fear and mistrust. Instead, acknowledge the emotion behind the belief and keep the relationship a source of safety, which makes it more likely they'll accept care.

What do I do in a psychosis crisis?

If there's immediate risk of harm, call or text 988 or dial 911, describe the situation plainly, and tell responders it's a mental health crisis. Safety comes first.
