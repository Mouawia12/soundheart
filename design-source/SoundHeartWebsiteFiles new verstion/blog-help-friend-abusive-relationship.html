Supporting Someone You Love

How to Help a Friend in an Abusive Relationship (Without Pushing Them Away)

By Nawal Ibrahim Alhawsawi, LPC · LMFT, SoundHeart Counseling · 6 min read

If you are in crisis, please don't wait. Call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or dial 911. SoundHeart is not a crisis or emergency service. This article is educational and is not a substitute for individual clinical care.

Watching someone you love stay in a relationship that's hurting them is agonizing, especially when they can't or won't see it yet. The instinct is to give ultimatums or force the issue. But the most powerful thing you can usually do is stay a safe, non-judgmental person they can come back to.

Why "just leave" backfires

Leaving an abusive relationship is rarely simple or safe, it's often the most dangerous time. Abuse also works by isolating the person and eroding their confidence. If your support comes with pressure or judgment, you risk becoming one more person they have to hide from, which is exactly what an abuser wants.

What helps instead

  • Say what you see, gently and without ultimatums: "The way they speak to you worries me. You deserve to feel safe."
  • Believe them and don't minimize. Avoid "why do you stay?", ask "how are you doing?"
  • Keep the door open. "I'm here, no matter what you decide." Stay reachable.
  • Help them stay connected to the outside world, so isolation doesn't win.
  • Let them make their own choices, on their own timeline, autonomy is part of healing.

Know the resources

You don't have to be the expert. You can gently point toward specialized help like the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233), which offers confidential safety planning. If there's immediate danger, that's a 911 situation.

Protect yourself, too

Being someone's lifeline is heavy, and it can stir up your own history. Sometimes what surfaces are your own old patterns. Tending to your own support keeps you steady enough to keep showing up.

Common questions

How do I help a friend in an abusive relationship?

Stay a safe, non-judgmental person. Name what you see gently, believe them, avoid ultimatums, keep the door open, help them stay connected to others, and point them toward specialized resources, while letting them make their own decisions.

Why doesn't my friend just leave?

Leaving is often the most dangerous time, and abuse works by isolating and undermining the person. It's rarely a simple choice, which is why pressure and judgment tend to backfire.

What resources can I share?

The National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) offers confidential support and safety planning. If there's immediate danger, call 911.
